# Giving & Receiving Feedback

## Overview

Feedback is information about past behavior or work that helps someone adjust and improve. Giving it well and receiving it well are core to performance, learning, and relationships—and to productivity (yours and your team’s).

## Why It Matters

- **Performance** — People improve when they know what’s working and what to change.
- **Alignment** — Expectations and standards become clear; rework decreases.
- **Trust** — Honest, kind feedback builds respect; see [Networking and Relationship Building](./Networking-Relationship-Building.md) and [Boundaries](./Boundaries.md).
- **Growth** — Receiving feedback supports [Mindset](../Self-Management/Mindset.md) and [Learning & Knowledge Management](../Personal-Development/Learning-Knowledge-Management.md).

## Giving Feedback

### Be Specific

- Describe behavior or output, not character: “The report was missing the cost section” not “You’re careless.”
- Give an example: “In the last two meetings you interrupted when X was speaking.”
- Makes it clear what to repeat or change.

### Timely and Regular

- Close to the event when possible, so context is fresh.
- Don’t save everything for one big review; small, frequent feedback is easier to act on.
- See [Review & Reflection Systems](../Self-Management/Review-Reflection-Systems.md) for rhythm (e.g. 1:1s, reviews).

### Balance

- Include what’s working, not only what’s wrong.
- “Start with one thing that went well” can make the rest easier to hear.
- Still be clear about what needs to change.

### Focus on Impact and Change

- “When you do X, the impact is Y” (on the team, project, or customer).
- Suggest or agree on one concrete next step when possible.
- Invite their view: “How do you see it?” See [Effective Listening](./Effective-Listening.md).

### Choose Setting

- Praise can be public if the person is okay with it; criticism is usually private.
- Sensitive or big topics: choose a setting that allows dialogue; see [Conflict Resolution](./Conflict-Resolution.md).

## Receiving Feedback

### Listen First

- Don’t interrupt or defend immediately; see [Effective Listening](./Effective-Listening.md).
- Assume good intent; feedback is data, not an attack.
- Ask for examples if it’s vague: “Can you give me an example?”

### Clarify and Reflect

- Paraphrase: “So you’re saying I could improve X by doing Y?”
- Take time to process before deciding what to do; you don’t have to agree on the spot.
- Use [Review & Reflection Systems](../Self-Management/Review-Reflection-Systems.md) to note patterns over time.

### Decide What to Use

- You don’t have to act on every piece of feedback.
- Weigh it: source, context, your goals; see [Decision-Making Frameworks](../Personal-Development/Decision-Making-Frameworks.md) and [Bias](../Self-Management/bias/bias.md).
- Thank them for the input either way; it encourages future feedback.

### Growth Mindset

- Treat feedback as a chance to learn; see [Mindset](../Self-Management/Mindset.md).
- Ask for feedback proactively when you’re working on something specific.

## 360-Degree and Structured Feedback

- **360 feedback** — From manager, peers, reports (and sometimes self). Use for patterns, not one comment.
- **Structured reviews** — Performance or project reviews; prepare examples and focus on behavior and impact.
- **Regular 1:1s** — Space for ongoing feedback and alignment; see [Review & Reflection Systems](../Self-Management/Review-Reflection-Systems.md) and [Meeting Effectiveness](../Productivity-Techniques/Meeting-Effectiveness.md).
