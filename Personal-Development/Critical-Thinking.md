# Critical Thinking

## Overview

Critical thinking is the disciplined practice of deciding what to believe or do. It combines clear questions, reliable evidence, sound reasoning, and a willingness to revise conclusions.

## A Practical Process

1. **Define the question.** Replace a vague concern with a specific, answerable question.
2. **Clarify terms.** Identify words that different people may interpret differently.
3. **Separate claims from evidence.** A confident statement is still a claim until supported.
4. **Assess the source.** Check relevant expertise, methods, incentives, independence, and recency.
5. **Look for alternatives.** Ask what else could explain the observation.
6. **Test the reasoning.** Check whether the conclusion follows and whether important assumptions are hidden.
7. **Calibrate confidence.** Use degrees of confidence rather than forcing certainty.
8. **Update.** State what new evidence would change the conclusion.

## Evaluating Evidence

- Prefer original data, primary documents, and systematic evidence when available.
- Distinguish anecdotes, expert judgment, observational data, and controlled experiments.
- Check the comparison group, sample, measurement, timeframe, and base rate.
- Do not confuse correlation with causation.
- Look for missing information and failed attempts, not only visible successes.
- Triangulate consequential claims across independent sources.

## Evaluating Arguments

Write the reasoning in a simple form:

- **Claim:** What is being asserted?
- **Evidence:** What observations support it?
- **Warrant:** Why should that evidence support this claim?
- **Assumptions:** What must be true for the argument to hold?
- **Alternatives:** What competing explanations fit the evidence?

## Useful Questions

- What problem are we actually solving?
- How do we know this?
- What would we expect to observe if the claim were false?
- Is the evidence representative or merely memorable?
- Who benefits if this conclusion is accepted?
- What is the strongest reasonable objection?
- Are we disagreeing about facts, values, definitions, or priorities?
- What decision is reversible, and what is the cost of waiting?

## Productive Disagreement

- Restate the other position well enough that its holder recognizes it.
- Identify shared facts and shared goals before debating conclusions.
- Critique the reasoning, not the person.
- Make uncertainty and value judgments explicit.
- Treat changing your mind as successful learning.

## Common Failure Modes

- Starting with a preferred answer and searching only for support
- Treating credentials as a substitute for evidence
- Demanding perfect evidence from one side but not the other
- Using a single metric as the whole definition of success
- Confusing a vivid example with a typical outcome
- Continuing analysis when the decision is cheap and reversible
